You know what, kiddo? We want you to have fun working on this assignment. We ALWAYS want students to derive a modicum of pleasure from what we do in class, even if it’s tedious or challenging.

We suspect students read best when they’re allowed to select a book, that they work best when the specifics of the assignment adhere to their own design. For this independent reading unit, you’ll pick the book, pick the product, and help decide how it’s graded.

The work you submit should be singular. It ought to stand by itself, just outside the ordinary. It should be unusual, uncommon, even strange.

It also has an audience. If you’re comfortable with the prospect, finished work will go on display in the library, beside or within the book, for the next reader to find. Make something that tells them whether they should pick it up or leave it alone.

Your book is the only element that’s set in stone. Once you choose it, all the particulars are up to you.

Here’s how it works: you’ll read and log your thinking in the Brain Bunker; choose a lens and a product; hit your benchmarks; and design the rubric you’ll be graded on.

Let’s get it!

The Brain Bunker

What the Brain Bunker Is

The Brain Bunker is a private record of your reading. Only you and your teacher will see it.

I don't want a book report here! Don't summarize, don't write out a checklist, don't fixate on literary elements and narrative structure. Instead, record captivating moments: lines that make you stop reading, questions you still can't answer, anything that annoyed you, any word you just can't forget.

Your teacher will read your log; sometimes, they'll even write back.

Ground Rules

  • Start every entry from a page in your book. Write down the page number.
  • No summaries. An entry that only retells what happened doesn't count.
  • Use at least three different entry types before the project ends.
  • Aim for at least two entries a week.
  • The log is graded complete or incomplete. It counts toward your Effort score. Easy points, kids!

Entry Types

  • Stopped me: a line that landed. Copy it and say why.
  • Bugged me: something that annoyed, confused, or bothered you.
  • Reminded me: a connection to your life, a song, a show, the news.
  • Wondered: a question the book raised and didn't answer.
  • Called it: a prediction, and whether you were right.
  • Would steal: a writing move you'd borrow for your own work.

What an Entry Can Look Like

  • A photo of the page with sticky notes or pen marks (IF YOU OWN THE BOOK!)
  • A voice memo, 30 seconds or so
  • A quick sketch
  • A screenshot with a quote and a sentence about it
  • A song link with one line on why
  • A few written sentences

Mix it up! Whatever you choose, include a specific page number.

Choosing Your Product

Pick a Lens, Then a Product

Every product starts with a question about your book. We'll call these questions lenses.

  • How this book works
  • Who this book is for/who should skip it
  • Where this book meets me
  • What else could happen
  • Talking back
  • The world of the book

Choose the lens that best fits what you want to say; then choose a product within it. You'll decide by the end of week one and you can switch at the midpoint conference.

How this book works

Analytical Review

Prefer a more traditional assignment? This is the way to go! Compose a quick summary (no more than three sentences!) of your book’s plot, then spend some time analyzing and interpreting its content. Your final product can be a podcast, a polished piece of writing, a video essay, or something in-between.

Spec: 700 to 1,000 words, or a 5 to 8 minute podcast or video essay. Ground your analysis in at least three specific passages.

Exhibit

Curate a small museum exhibit for your book. Choose five objects, real or imagined, that unlock how the book works: a prop, a setting, a symbol, a document a character would own.

Spec: an introductory panel of 100 to 150 words, plus five object labels of 60 to 120 words each. Each label names the object, points to where it appears, and explains why it matters.

Who this book is for

Shelf Talk

Booksellers tuck small handwritten cards beside books they love. They're called shelf talkers. Make one for your book, then go longer.

Spec: a shelf-talker card of 40 to 75 words for the library display, plus one longer review: a 60 to 90 second video or 300 to 500 written words. Your review must answer two questions: Who should read this? Who should skip it, and why?

Playlist

Curate a playlist based on the characters, content, and/or culture of your book! Annotate each song, explaining why you select it and connecting it to other tracks in your queue. Remember that a good DJ is a storyteller! Think about the thematic content of lyrics, overall emotion of each song, and your set’s sense of pace. People want to dance all night—lend a hand!

Spec: 8 to 12 tracks. Two to four sentences of annotation per track. A liner note of 100 to 150 words that introduces the set and explains its order.

Where this book meets me

Your Way In

Explain how you relate to the story. This should take the form of an introspective consideration of both your life and the book’s content. Where do they converge, coming together? Where do they diverge, pulling apart? What connections are most important? Where did you find common ground?

Spec: 700 to 1,000 words, or 5 to 7 minutes of audio or video. Anchor your reflection in at least three passages from the book.

What else could happen

Alternative Ending

Devise another ending to the book. Bear in mind that your conclusion might require some adjustments to the plot! Cut as much as you want, and let us know where you imagine your version taking over from the original (chapter, page number, etc.).

Spec: 1,200 to 2,000 words, plus an author's note of 100 to 150 words on what you changed and why.

Fanfiction

Fanfiction is a type of writing that uses existing intellectual property, like characters and settings, as the basis for a new piece of writing. So long as your fic demonstrates an understanding of your book, you can go wherever you want with it.

Spec: 1,200 to 2,000 words, plus an author's note of 100 to 150 words naming what from the book your story depends on.

Talking back

Author Interview

Imagine a list of at least ten questions you’d like to ask the author of your book. Remember that good interviewers do their homework and have their own opinions! See what a little research on your author reveals and start from there.

Spec: 10 to 12 questions, each followed by one sentence on what prompted it. Consult at least three sources about the author and cite them in MLA style.

Media Appearance

Bring your book's world into the media. Book a character on a talk show, or cover the story as news.

Spec, choose one:

  • A talk show script of 4 to 6 pages, or a 5 to 7 minute performance, live or recorded.
  • A feature, profile, or opinion column of 600 to 900 words that treats the book's world as real.
  • An advertisement for a good or service from the book's world: one printed page or a 30 to 60 second video, plus a 100 to 150 word note on what it reveals about the book.

Talk Show Whether you want to go classy or trashy, a talk show is a surefire way to capture an audience’s attention. Imagine how your characters could come to life in front of a live crowd, then draft a script to make it happen. You can either perform for your peers in class or record something ahead of time.

Periodical Turn in a newspaper or magazine article related to your book! Possible angles include opinion columns reacting to an event, feature articles profiling a fictional character as though they were a real person, or an advertisement for a featured good or service.

The world of the book

Menu

Craft a lavish, five-course dinner based on the characters, content, and/or culture of your book! Select ingredients, cooking techniques, and beverages that relate to key elements of the text. Remember that atmosphere—decor, environment, place settings, and service—are an essential aspect of dining out.

Spec: five courses and a drink, each with a 50 to 100 word description tied to the book. Add a note of about 100 words on the setting: decor, place settings, service. Cooking is optional.

Map

Map the places that matter in your book, real or invented.

Spec: 6 to 10 locations, each with a 50 to 100 word annotation tied to a page. Draw it by hand or build it digitally.

Any lens

Your Brilliant Idea

Want to do something outside the box? Let’s talk!

Spec: you set it. Your scope statement at the midpoint conference defines what you'll make, and your teacher approves it. Games and graphic novels live here.

Formats (Optional)

Any product can take any form: a zine, a video, a podcast, a slide deck, a branching story, a digital map. Tools are optional (and sometimes involve a learning curve). Choose one only if it helps you say what you want to say.

Benchmarks and Scope

Benchmarks

  1. Week 1:Choose your book, your lens, and a tentative product. Explain your choice in a paragraph or voice memo.
  2. Week 2:First Brain Bunker check. Find one real-world model you admire and share the link.
  3. Midpoint:Conference with your teacher. Confirm or switch your product. Submit your scope statement.
  4. Week 4:Prototype. One page, a 30-second clip, three courses, five tracks.
  5. Week 5:Full draft. Finalize your rubric using the criterion bank.
  6. Final week:Showcase, self-assessment, and display.

Your Scope Statement

At the midpoint, complete this sentence:

My product will be ________, and it will contain ________.

Your Execution score measures how well you deliver what you promised, not how big it gets. Going past the spec earns no extra credit. One thing done well beats three things done halfway.

Plan Early, Build Late

You choose your product in week one, but don't start building until after your midpoint conference! Until then, your job is reading and logging. The log is where your product's best ideas will come from.

How Your Rubric Works

Design Your Rubric

Your rubric has six rows. Four are set by your teacher. Choose the other two from the bank below and rewrite them to fit your product.

For every row, write one description of what success looks like. Make sure it's specific and relevant to your product. "The playlist flows" is vague. "Each track's mood sets up the next and the order follows the book's plot" is specific.

You and your teacher will separately score every row. Those two scores are averaged to determine your final grade.

Reading the Rubric

Each row has three columns:

  • Needs work: where your product falls short of the description
  • Success looks like: your description
  • Went beyond: where your product exceeds it

You and your teacher write notes in the outer columns, then assign points for the row.

A Word on Self-Assessment

In my experience, student self-assessment leads to polarized responses: you either give yourself top marks or grade a letter below what your teacher thinks you deserve. Know thyself, reader, and try to render an honest evaluation of the work you've actually done. Honesty is rewarded; deceit, even self-deceit, eventually collapses like a botched soufflé.

The Fixed Rows

10 points

The Content

(the extent to which your work exhibits sound understanding, interpretation, and/or analysis of its subject)

Needs work
Managing
  • Some grasp of the book; little blending of evidence and interpretation
  • Some use of specific passages
Struggling
  • Few/no accurate references to the book
  • Ideas are not developed
Failing
  • Student work somehow shows no sign of the book
Success looks like

Your product shows you read closely and understood what you read. It draws on at least three specific passages and it demonstrates an understanding of the book.

Went beyond
Excelling
  • Establishes a strong, accurate grasp of the book's plot/setting/characters/ideas
  • Develops insight through specific passages, details, or examples
10 points

The Performance

(the extent to which your work is skillfully executed, presenting specific and relevant material to its audience)

Needs work
Managing
  • Develops ideas briefly; uses some detail
Struggling
  • Uses incomplete or undeveloped details
Failing
  • Doesn’t contain a single idea.
Success looks like

Your product delivers what your scope statement promised and meets the spec for your product. It is finished, polished, and on time.

Went beyond
Excelling
  • Develops ideas clearly and fully; uses a wide range of relevant details
5 points

Your Effort

(the extent to which you exercise classroom citizenship and self-discipline)

Needs work
Managing
  • Student completed some previous classwork and activities.
Struggling
  • Student completed fragments of previous classwork and activities.
Failing
  • Student work is plagiarized.
  • Student didn’t do squat.
Success looks like

You kept up with your Brain Bunker and met every benchmark: book and lens in week one, a model in week two, a scope statement at the midpoint, a prototype, and a full draft.

Went beyond
Excelling
  • Student completed all previous classwork and activities.
5 points

School's Nitpicks

(the extent to which your work exhibits conventional grammar, spelling, word usage, etc.)

Needs work
Managing
  • Student work contains a significant number of grammatical errors.
  • Work contains incomplete or poorly structured thoughts.
Struggling
  • Student work is unclear and ungrammatical throughout.
Failing
  • Student work is incoherent.
Success looks like

Your work is clear, direct, and free of errors in grammar, spelling, and word usage. Audio and video count, too: think about whether there's a need to provide scripts, captions, and/or on-screen text.

Went beyond
Excelling
  • Student work is clear, direct, engaging, and free of grammatical errors.
  • Work is artfully composed.

The Criterion Bank

General

The Blueprint

(the extent to which your work exhibits direction, shape, and coherence)

Needs work
Managing
  • Establishes but does not always maintain an appropriate focus; some inconsistencies in sequence of ideas
  • Meets some of the requirements specified for this assignment
Struggling
  • Lacks an appropriate focus, but suggests some organization
  • Meets few/no requirements specified for this assignment
Failing
  • Student work is a total mishmash
Success looks like

Every part has a job, and the parts add up to a whole. A newcomer can follow it from start to finish.

Went beyond
Excelling
  • Maintains a clear focus; exhibits a logical, coherent structure through appropriate transitions
  • Exceeds all requirements specified for this assignment
General

The Crowd

(the extent to which your work reveals an awareness of audience and purpose)

Needs work
Managing
  • Some use of concrete language, literary devices, and sensory detail in assignment
  • Some effective word choices
  • Occasional use of sentence variety
  • Some awareness of voice and audience
Struggling
  • Little use of concrete language, literary devices or sensory detail in assignment
  • Few effective word choices
  • Little sentence variety
  • Mechanical/unsuitable voice; unaware of audience
Failing
  • Student just dumped nonsensical words into a document.
Success looks like

The product speaks to a specific audience. Its tone, length, and style suit the people it's for.

Went beyond
Excelling
  • Creative, concrete language; uses literary devices and rich sensory detail
  • Uses sophisticated, precise vocabulary
  • Well varied sentence structure throughout
  • Unique voice; strong sense of audience
Criteria for This Lens

How this book works

  • Names a choice the author made and explains what it does to the reader.
  • Traces a pattern across the book: an image, a word, a symbol, a structure.
  • Shows how the book's form shapes its meaning.
Criteria for This Lens

Who this book is for

  • Names a specific reader and gives them a real reason to pick up the book.
  • Warns honestly, with evidence, about who should skip it.
  • Matches its tone to the people it's trying to reach.
Criteria for This Lens

Where this book meets me

  • Connects the book and your life in both directions.
  • Uses specific memories and details, not generalities.
  • Admits where the connection breaks down.
Criteria for This Lens

What else could happen

  • Keeps the characters' voices true to the book.
  • Makes changes that matter, with believable consequences.
  • Explains your choices in an author's note.
Criteria for This Lens

Talking back

  • Shows research: questions or claims an uninformed fan couldn't make.
  • Takes a position and defends it.
  • Treats the book's world consistently, as if it were real.
Criteria for This Lens

The world of the book

  • Ties every item, place, or course to a specific page.
  • Builds atmosphere through sensory detail.
  • Organizes the world so a newcomer can find their way around it.
Format

Writing

  • Has a distinct voice suited to the product.
  • Moves logically from part to part.
  • Uses precise, vivid language.
Format

Audio and Video

  • Holds attention with deliberate pacing.
  • Uses sound, music, or images that support the content.
  • Sounds or looks clean enough to easily follow.
Format

Visual and Print

  • Uses layout to guide the eye.
  • Makes images and text work together.
  • Holds up on display, from a few feet away.
Format

Live Performance

  • Shows rehearsal: delivery is confident and timed.
  • Uses voice, movement, and props with purpose.
  • Keeps the audience engaged throughout.
Format

Digital Tools

  • Works: links, branches, and pins all function.
  • Is easy to navigate for someone who hasn't seen it.
  • Uses the tool because it helps, not for show.
